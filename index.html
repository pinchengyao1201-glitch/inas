1

Choose a sector

Which part of the economy am I looking at?

The stock market is divided into 11 sectors that group companies by what they sell: technology, healthcare, energy, financials, utilities, consumer staples, consumer discretionary, industrials, materials, real estate, and communication services.

Sectors react differently to the economic cycle. When interest rates rise, utilities and real estate tend to suffer because their borrowing costs climb. During an expansion, industrials and consumer discretionary usually lead. When recession fears grow, healthcare and consumer staples hold up, because people still buy medicine and groceries. A strong company in the wrong sector can still be a losing investment, so INAS starts here instead of with a ticker.

Microsoft case

We chose technology, the largest sector in the S&P 500 by weight. Its main tailwind right now is spending on AI: businesses are buying the computing power, software, and cloud capacity that technology companies sell.

2

Read the macro environment

What are rates, inflation, and growth doing to that sector?

No company controls the economy, but every company is affected by it. INAS tracks five areas, and each one reaches a company's earnings or valuation through a specific channel.

Microsoft case

For technology, interest rates matter most. Much of a tech company's value comes from earnings expected years from now, and higher rates discount those earnings more heavily. That is why fast-growing tech stocks often fall further than mature companies when rates rise.

3

Narrow to an industry

Which business model inside the sector do I want to own?

A sector contains industries with very different business models. Technology includes semiconductors, enterprise software, consumer hardware, IT services, internet platforms, and cloud infrastructure.

Semiconductors are cyclical: they boom when chip demand spikes and slump when inventory piles up. Enterprise software is steadier, because businesses pay recurring subscriptions and switching providers is costly. Consumer hardware follows device upgrade cycles. Choosing an industry means choosing which of these dynamics you want to be exposed to.

4

Analyze the company

Are the numbers strong, and will its advantages last?

Phase 4 looks at the company through two lenses. The financial statements show how well it makes money today. A SWOT analysis asks whether its advantages will last. Each lens covers the other's blind spot: strong numbers without a lasting advantage can fade, while a durable advantage can make temporarily weak numbers worth buying.

5

Map the competitors

Who could take its customers, and is its lead holding?

No company competes alone. Phase 5 identifies the main rivals in each part of the business and compares them on what decides winners: market share, growth, margins, and positioning. The goal is to understand where the company wins, why, and whether that advantage will last.

It also looks past current rivals to potential entrants, companies that could move into the market, and substitutes, products that meet the same need in a different way. Many investors assume today's market share is permanent. This phase tests that assumption.

Microsoft case

Azure has less market share than AWS but is growing faster. Many businesses already use Windows and Office, so when they choose a cloud provider, staying with Microsoft is often the easiest option.

6

Allocate the portfolio

How much of my money does this idea deserve?

The last phase turns individual analysis into a portfolio decision. After running INAS across several sectors and companies, you decide how much weight each position deserves. A company you understand well, with a clear thesis and limited downside, might earn 15 to 20 percent of the portfolio. One with an exciting story but more uncertainty might earn 5 percent.

Allocation also means thinking about correlation. Holding three tech companies and one energy company is not real diversification, because a bad quarter for tech hurts most of the portfolio at once. That is why INAS is meant to be run across several sectors.

Exhibit 7Example allocation across four sectors

Technology35%
Healthcare25%
Financials20%
Utilities15%
Cash5%

Illustrative only. In a high-rate environment, defensive sectors such as healthcare and utilities would get more weight; in an expansion, growth sectors such as technology would get more.

What INAS can't do yet

What happens when the numbers and the story disagree?

Microsoft is an easy case: strong numbers and a clear advantage. So I tested INAS on a harder one. CoreWeave brought in $5.1 billion in revenue in 2025 but lost $1.17 billion. It also reported $66.8 billion in expected future revenue from customer contracts.

In Phase 4, the loss counted against CoreWeave, while its customer contracts pointed to future growth. Investors weren't ignoring the loss; they were betting that future demand would justify it. INAS had no way to weigh that bet. If it struggles with an established company, it cannot yet evaluate a startup with little financial history.

Exhibit 8CoreWeave, fiscal year 2025

Revenue$5.1B
Net income−$1.17B
Expected future revenue from contracts$66.8B
What INAS sawA company losing more than a billion dollars a year.
What investors sawContracted demand worth about thirteen times its annual revenue.
The gapINAS can tell strong numbers from weak ones, but it has no way to weigh what the numbers show against what investors believe a company could become. That is what the next version needs to do.
